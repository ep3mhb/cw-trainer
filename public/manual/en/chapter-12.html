Chapter 12Glossary

12.1Every term the application puts on screen

Dit and dah
The two elements Morse is built from — the short one and the long one. Say them aloud as "dit" and "dah" rather than "dot" and "dash": Morse is a sound, and naming it as a sound is how it stays one.
Element
One dit or one dah. The letter C (dah-di-dah-dit) is four elements.
WPM (words per minute)
Speed, measured against the standard word PARIS, which takes exactly 50 dit-lengths to send. At 20 WPM a dit is 60 milliseconds, because 1200 divided by 20 is 60.
Character speed
How fast the characters themselves are sent. This is the rhythm you are learning to recognise.
Effective speed
The overall speed once the gaps between characters are counted. Always the same as, or slower than, the character speed.
Farnsworth spacing
Sending the characters quickly but leaving extra silence between them. You hear each character at its true, final rhythm from the first lesson, while the gaps give you time to think. As you improve you shorten the gaps rather than relearning the characters.
Koch method
Start at full speed with just two characters, and add one more only when you are copying at 90%. The alternative — learning all forty slowly and speeding up — teaches you to count elements, a habit that has to be painfully unlearned around 10 WPM.
Weighting
The length of a dah measured in dits. Textbook is exactly 3. A heavier weighting (3.2, say) sounds more deliberate and is often easier to copy; lighter sounds hurried. Your own weighting is one of the things that makes your sending recognisable.
Fist
Your personal sending style — your weighting, your spacing, your hesitations. A good fist is even and easy to copy. Operators genuinely recognise each other by fist alone.
Sidetone
The tone you hear while keying. It is generated locally and is not transmitted anywhere.
Envelope (rise and fall)
How quickly the tone fades up and down at the start and end of each element. Switching it on and off instantly produces a click that is tiring to listen to and splatters across the band on the air. Four to eight milliseconds is right.
Latency
The delay between pressing the key and hearing the tone. Under about 60 ms you stop noticing; much above it you are practising against a delay that will not be there on the air. Bluetooth audio adds 100–200 ms and cannot be fixed in software.
Decoder tolerance
How forgiving the app is when judging your sending. Loose while you are starting out, tightened as you improve so it starts holding you to real timing.
Consistency
How even your elements are across a whole run. Being consistently slightly wrong is far easier to copy than being erratically almost right, which is why this is scored separately from accuracy.
Confusion matrix
A grid of what was sent against what you typed. It turns "I keep making mistakes" into "I hear S as H", which you can actually drill.
Prosign
Two letters run together with no gap between them, acting as a piece of punctuation rather than letters — see the list further down.
QSO
A contact — one complete exchange between two stations.
RST
The signal report: readability 1–5, strength 1–9, tone 1–9. "599" means perfectly readable, extremely strong, perfect tone — and is sent so routinely that it often means little.
Callsign
The identifier issued to a licensed station, such as A65XY. It is yours alone and it is a legal requirement to identify with it on the air.
Zero-beat
Tuning your dial exactly onto another station, so it sounds at your own sidetone pitch. The skill the On air tab teaches.
Passband
The slice of the band your receiver filter lets through, shown as the lighter strip either side of your dial on the scope.
Waterfall
The scrolling picture of the band over time under the spectrum: bright where a signal is keying, dark where there is only noise.
Training identifier
An A0 identifier issued to a member without a licence, so that copying it is the same exercise as a callsign. Never a real callsign; never exported.
Over
One station’s turn to transmit. Contacts on the band are over to over: your key is held while the other station sends.
ADIF
Amateur Data Interchange Format — the plain-text log format every logging program imports.
Pile-up
Several stations on or near one frequency. The scope labels the strongest two and counts the rest.
